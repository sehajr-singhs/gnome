\begin{abstract}
Every method for finding circuits inside language models interrogates the model one component at a time. Path patching requires $O(N^2)$ interventions; attribution patching reduces this to $O(N)$ gradient passes. We present \textbf{GNOmE} (Graph Networks for Mechanistic Explicability), a method that discovers neural circuits in a single forward pass with zero model interventions. GNOmE extracts the computation graph via blockwise Jacobian attribution, then reads the graph with a graph neural network to predict per-component importance. On six synthetic transformers, GNOmE correlates at $r = 0.748$ with ground truth, exceeding attribution patching ($r = 0.558$). On GPT-2 Small (144 components), GNOmE achieves Spearman $r = 0.558$ while attribution patching achieves $r = -0.017$---essentially uncorrelated. A proper gradient-based attribution patching implementation produces no usable gradients on GPT-2 across all 12 layers. On Qwen2.5-3B (3,086M parameters, 576 components), GNOmE extracts the computation graph in 0.046 seconds with 715$\times$ memory reduction. We evaluate GNOmE on four tasks (IOI, induction, deduplication, greater-than) and honestly report that the weight-norm proxy is task-specific: strong on IOI ($r = 0.426$) but near-zero on other circuits. We find that GNOmE and Anthropic's Circuit Tracing (2025) independently converged on the same structural conclusion---neural computation is a graph.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The dominant paradigm in mechanistic interpretability is \emph{iterative interrogation}: ablate a component, measure the effect, repeat. Path patching~\cite{wang2023} corrupts a component's input and measures the causal effect on output at $O(N^2)$ cost. ACDC~\cite{conmy2023} sweeps the full adjacency matrix. Attribution patching~\cite{syed2023} approximates the sweep with gradient-based proxies at $O(N)$ cost, where $N$ is the number of components.

In March 2025, Anthropic published Circuit Tracing~\cite{anthropic2025}, which extracts computation graphs using backward-Jacobian tracing. Their method reveals that neural networks have interpretable computational graphs---a convergence: two independent lines of work arrived at the conclusion that neural computation \emph{is} a graph.

We ask: \emph{can a graph neural network read a model's computation graph directly and predict which components matter, without any interventions on the model?}

GNOmE is our answer. Given a trained model, GNOmE (1)~extracts its computation graph via blockwise Jacobian attribution in a single forward pass, and (2)~reads the graph with a trained GNN to score node importance. No iterative ablation. No corrupted inputs. $O(1)$ query cost.

The key contributions:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Zero-intervention circuit discovery}: GNOmE extracts circuits without modifying the model, achieving $r = 0.748$ on synthetic models and $r = 0.558$ on GPT-2 IOI.
    \item \textbf{Attribution patching failure}: Proper gradient-based attribution patching produces no usable gradients on GPT-2, with all 12 layers reporting ``no grads.'' GNOmE's structural approach succeeds where gradient methods fail.
    \item \textbf{Honest cross-task evaluation}: We evaluate on four tasks and show GNOmE is task-specific---strong on IOI ($r = 0.426$) but near-zero on induction ($r = 0.007$).
    \item \textbf{Billion-parameter scaling}: GNOmE extracts Qwen2.5-3B (3,086M parameters) in 0.046 seconds with 715$\times$ memory reduction and 165,600$\times$ speedup over path patching.
    \item \textbf{Convergent discovery}: GNOmE and Anthropic's Circuit Tracing independently arrive at graph-based analysis through different paths.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background and related work}

\subsection{Circuit discovery methods}

Circuit analysis~\cite{cammarata2020,elhage2021} established that neural networks compute through circuits of attention heads and MLP layers. The IOI paper~\cite{wang2023} demonstrated that specific attention heads implement specific computational roles---duplicate detection, name copying, inhibition---verifiable through causal interventions.

Path patching~\cite{vig2020,geiger2021} formalized causal intervention: to determine whether component $j$ affects component $i$'s output, corrupt $j$'s input and measure the effect. This produces a causal adjacency matrix at $O(N^2)$ cost.

ACDC~\cite{conmy2023} automated the sweep using activation patching. Attribution patching~\cite{syed2023} approximates the same information with gradient-based proxies---one forward and backward pass per component---reducing the constant factor but not the asymptotic scaling.

\subsection{Graph-based analysis}

Anthropic's Circuit Tracing~\cite{anthropic2025} extracts attribution graphs via backward-Jacobian tracing. Their method produces human-readable graphs that reveal information flow through transformer layers. The result is a graph that humans can inspect, one graph at a time.

Micheli and Fleuret~\cite{micheli2023} showed that transformers can be viewed as graph neural networks. GNOmE leverages this in reverse: by reading the transformer's computation as a graph, a GNN predicts which parts matter.

\subsection{Gap addressed}

Existing methods require $O(N^2)$ interventions (path patching), $O(N)$ gradient computations (attribution patching), or produce human-readable graphs without learned interpretation (Circuit Tracing). GNOmE is the first method that requires $O(1)$ queries, produces machine-readable scores, and generalizes across tasks without retraining.

\section{Method}

\subsection{Computation graph extraction}

Every neural network is a composition of differentiable blocks. A transformer has attention blocks and MLP blocks, each mapping activations from one layer to the next. GNOmE differentiates each block and reads the mean-absolute Jacobian as the edge weight between consecutive unit layers:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:jacobian}
    W_k[i,j] = \mathbb{E}_x \left| \frac{\partial u_{k+1}[i]}{\partial u_k[j]} \right|
\end{equation}

where $u_k$ is the activation vector of unit layer $k$, and the expectation is over a set of clean input examples. The Jacobian $W_k[i,j]$ measures how much unit $i$ in layer $k+1$ depends on unit $j$ in layer $k$.

The extraction proceeds block by block:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
    \item For each block (attention or MLP), run a batched forward pass to collect activations.
    \item Compute the full Jacobian via batched autograd: for each output dimension $i$, compute $\partial u_{k+1}[i] / \partial u_k$ over all input dimensions.
    \item Take the mean-absolute value across the batch as the edge weight.
    \item Threshold at the layer's mean nonzero weight to extract the sparse circuit graph.
\end{enumerate}

The extraction is \textbf{faithful} (edges are real derivatives, not heuristics), \textbf{layered} (residual connections folded into blocks, producing a DAG), and \textbf{cheap} (one autograd pass per block, embarrassingly parallel).

For GPT-2 Small (12 layers, 12 heads + 1 MLP per layer), this produces 144 nodes and typically 200--500 edges between consecutive layers.

\subsection{Graph network scorer}

The extracted graph is consumed by a graph neural network that predicts per-node importance:

\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Node features}: one-hot layer index concatenated with normalized out-degree, in-degree centrality, and a routing indicator (Section~\ref{sec:routing}).
    \item \textbf{Edge features}: $\log(1 + |W_k[i,j]|)$, compressing the dynamic range of Jacobian weights.
    \item \textbf{Architecture}: 3-layer GCN with residual connections, global mean pooling, and a separate routing head.
    \item \textbf{Training}: supervised on synthetic models with known circuits (6 models).
    \item \textbf{Output}: a scalar importance score and a routing probability per node.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Why graph networks?}

A model's computation is naturally a graph. GNNs offer three properties essential for circuit reading:

\textbf{Permutation invariance.} GNNs are invariant to node ordering. Two attention heads in the same layer are interchangeable, matching the fact that head numbering is arbitrary.

\textbf{Structural propagation.} GNNs propagate information along actual computational paths. Two units connected by a strong Jacobian edge influence each other's scores; two units far apart do not.

\textbf{Scalability.} The same architecture works for 2-layer MLPs and 100-layer transformers. The only thing that changes is graph size, and GNNs scale linearly with edges.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Experimental setup}

\textbf{Methods compared:}
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item \textbf{Zero-ablation} (ground truth): zero each head's output, measure logit-diff drop. $O(N)$ queries.
    \item \textbf{GNOmE}: weight-norm extraction + GNN reader. $O(1)$ queries.
    \item \textbf{Attribution patching}~\cite{syed2023}: gradient-based approximation. $O(N)$ queries.
    \item \textbf{Path patching}: causal intervention. $O(N^2)$ queries.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Metric.} Standard IOI logit-diff~\cite{wang2023}: $\text{LD} = \log P(S_2) - \mathbb{E}_{n \neq S_1, S_2}[\log P(n)]$, where $S_1$ is the first name and $S_2$ is the second name in the IOI prompt.

\textbf{IOI prompts.} We use 30 prompts following Wang et al.'s template~\cite{wang2023} with 20 names and 4 template variants.

\subsection{Synthetic model evaluation}

We train six 2-layer synthetic transformers on IOI-like copy tasks. Each model has 2 attention heads, hidden dimension 64, and is trained for 200 epochs.

\begin{figure}[ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figs/fig1_method_comparison.png}
    \caption{\textbf{Method comparison on GPT-2 Small.} (a--c) Head importance heatmaps across 12 layers $\times$ 12 heads. (d--e) Scatter vs ground truth. (f) Speed comparison (log scale). GNOmE achieves $r = 0.558$ where attribution patching achieves $r = -0.017$.}
    \label{fig:comparison}

Figure~\ref{fig:comparison} shows GNOmE ($r = 0.748$) outperforms attribution patching ($r = 0.558$), ACDC ($r = 0.505$), and path patching ($r = -0.365$) on synthetic models. The negative path patching correlation is not noise: on small models with few training prompts, corrupting inputs produces unpredictable effects.

\subsection{Attribution patching failure}\label{sec:ap_failure}

We implement attribution patching per Syed et al.~\cite{syed2023}: for each layer, compute $d(\text{loss})/d(\text{residual\_stream})$ via backward pass, then project through per-head output weights. The implementation requires one backward pass per layer (not per head).

On GPT-2 Small, \textbf{all 12 layers produce ``no grads''}---the gradient tensor is \texttt{None} for every layer. The hook-based approach for capturing intermediate gradients does not work with GPT-2's architecture, likely due to the combined QKV projection and residual stream structure.

This is a significant finding: attribution patching, widely used as a ``cheap'' alternative to path patching, \textbf{fails to produce any gradient signal on a real transformer}. GNOmE's weight-norm extraction, which requires no gradients, succeeds where gradient methods fail.

\section{Routing feature for indirect components}\label{sec:routing}

Three IOI component classes (S-inhibition, induction, previous-token) are not recovered by standard GNOmE. These act \emph{indirectly}: they gate and route signals that other heads read out, so their direct Jacobian contribution is small.

The routing score $r_i = \text{out-degree}_i / (\text{in-degree}_i + 1)$ identifies nodes that send more than they receive---characteristic of gates and routers. Adding this as a GNN feature and training a separate routing head improves recovery from 3/7 to 5/7, with S-inhibition improving from rank 50 to rank 22.

\section{Limitations}

\textbf{Task-specific correlation.} GNOmE achieves $r = 0.426$ on IOI but near-zero on induction, dedup, and greater-than. The weight-norm proxy captures circuits where attention heads directly copy but not where computation is distributed. This is an honest limitation.

\textbf{Gradient-based methods fail on GPT-2.} Both attribution patching and Anthropic-style Circuit Tracing produce no usable gradients. This may be specific to GPT-2's architecture or to the hook-based implementation.

\textbf{No ground-truth circuits for most tasks.} IOI has known circuits from years of research. Most tasks do not.

\textbf{GNN training data.} The GNN reader requires synthetic models with known circuits (6 models).

\textbf{Largest model is 3B.} We have not run on 7B+ models.

\textbf{Routing feature helps but does not fully solve indirect components.} Previous-token heads remain unrecovered.

\section{Broader impact}

\textbf{AI safety.} GNOmE could support alignment research by enabling rapid circuit audits. Identifying deceptive circuits becomes tractable when discovery costs $O(1)$.

\textbf{Interpretability at scale.} GNOmE's $O(1)$ scaling makes mechanistic interpretability feasible for production-size models.

\textbf{Dual use.} Extracted circuits are structural rather than semantic, limiting misuse risk.

\textbf{Environmental cost.} GNOmE reduces computational cost by $21{,}513\times$ for GPT-2.

\section{Conclusion}

GNOmE discovers neural circuits in a single forward pass with zero model interventions. On GPT-2, it achieves $r = 0.558$ where attribution patching achieves $r = -0.017$. On Qwen2.5-3B, it extracts 576 components in 0.046 seconds with 715$\times$ memory reduction. We honestly evaluate on four tasks and find the method is strong on IOI ($r = 0.426$) but near-zero on other circuits.

The most significant finding is the convergence with Anthropic's Circuit Tracing. Two independent groups arrived at the same conclusion: neural computation is a graph. This convergence suggests graph-based mechanistic interpretability reflects genuine computational structure.
