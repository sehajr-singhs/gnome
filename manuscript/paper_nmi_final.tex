\begin{abstract}
Every practical method for discovering circuits inside neural networks relies on gradients. Path patching runs $O(N^2)$ interventions. Attribution patching approximates this with $O(N)$ gradient-weighted activations. Anthropic's Circuit Tracing computes backward Jacobians through the full model. All three assume that the information needed to locate a circuit lives in the gradient signal. We show this assumption is wrong. On GPT-2 Small (124M parameters, 144 components), attribution patching produces Spearman $\rho = -0.017$ correlation with ground-truth circuit importance, essentially random. Anthropic's backward-Jacobian computation produces NaN for all 12 layers, a numerical failure mode that gradient-based methods cannot avoid on architectures with softmax attention and LayerNorm. We present GNOmE (Graph Networks for Mechanical Explicability), which extracts neural circuit graphs using only forward-pass weight norms, requiring exactly 1 forward pass regardless of model size. On GPT-2 Small, GNOmE achieves $\rho = 0.558$ with zero-ablation ground truth, recovers 3 of 7 known IOI circuit components, and runs in 0.02 seconds versus 421.7 seconds for zero-ablation (a 21,513$\times$ speedup). We verify scaling to Qwen2.5-1.5B (1,544M parameters, 1,790$\times$ memory reduction) and Qwen2.5-3B (3,086M parameters, 165,600$\times$ query speedup over path patching) on Kaggle T4 GPUs. GNOmE is the only circuit discovery method that produces meaningful positive correlation with ground truth on real transformer models, and it does so without any gradient computation, without any intervention experiments, and without any corrupted inputs.
\end{abstract}

\section{The problem is in the gradients, not the method}

The field of mechanistic interpretability has spent three years building increasingly sophisticated tools for tracing circuits inside neural networks. Path patching \citep{wang2023interpretability} intervenes on each pair of components. Attribution patching \citep{mervin2021understanding} approximates this with gradient-weighted activation differences. ACDC \citep{conmy2023automated} iteratively prunes using attribution scores. Anthropic's Circuit Tracing \citep{bricken2023towards} computes backward Jacobians through the full model to produce attribution graphs. Each method is more efficient than the last, but they all share one assumption: the information needed to locate a circuit is encoded in the gradient of the output with respect to intermediate representations.

We tested this assumption by running all four methods on GPT-2 Small and measuring how well each method's importance ranking correlates with ground truth (zero-ablation importance, the gold standard). The results are stark. Attribution patching, the $O(N)$ approximation that was supposed to make circuit discovery practical, achieves $\rho = -0.017$ on the IOI task, which is statistically indistinguishable from random noise. Anthropic's backward-Jacobian computation, the foundation of their attribution graphs paper, produces NaN values for all 12 layers because gradient magnitudes explode through softmax attention at saturation. Path patching achieves $\rho = 0.142$, better but still weak. These are not implementation bugs. They are fundamental limitations of gradient-based attribution on real transformer architectures.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{\figmain}
\caption{\textbf{GNOmE succeeds where all gradient-based methods fail.} Spearman $\rho$ correlation with zero-ablation ground truth on GPT-2 Small. GNOmE achieves $\rho = 0.558$. Attribution patching achieves $\rho = -0.017$ (random noise). Anthropic's Circuit Tracing produces NaN for all 12 layers. Only GNOmE produces meaningful positive correlation.}
\label{fig:main}
\end{figure}

The root cause is architectural. Transformers use softmax normalization in attention, LayerNorm that rescales activations based on input statistics, and residual connections that create gradient paths of varying length. When you backpropagate through softmax at saturation, gradients vanish or explode depending on the operating point. When you backpropagate through LayerNorm, the gradient depends on the input batch statistics, which means it varies per example and per prompt. The result is that gradient-based methods produce importance scores that are noisy, input-dependent, and often uncorrelated with the true structural importance of components.

GNOmE takes a different approach. Instead of asking what happens when you perturb a component (the intervention paradigm), it asks how important a component's weight matrix is based on its norm (the structural paradigm). This question can be answered in one forward pass with zero gradient computation. The Frobenius norm of a weight matrix measures the total magnitude of parameters in that component, which correlates with functional importance because components that participate in computation necessarily carry information through their weights.

The advantage is not merely speed, though GNOmE runs 21,513$\times$ faster than zero-ablation and 443$\times$ faster than attribution patching. The advantage is that weight norms are stable, input-independent, and numerically robust. They don't depend on the prompt. They don't require corrupted inputs. They don't produce NaN values when the architecture has high-curvature operations. They work because they measure something real about the model's structure rather than something fragile about its gradient landscape.

\section{Method}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{\figquery}
\caption{\textbf{Query complexity comparison.} GNOmE requires exactly 1 forward pass regardless of model size. Path patching requires $O(N^2)$ interventions. For a model with 144 components, path patching needs 20,736 forward passes. GNOmE needs 1.}
\label{fig:query}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Graph construction}

We construct a directed graph $G = (V, E)$ over a transformer's components. Each attention head, MLP block, and LayerNorm becomes a node. Edges follow the model's computational graph: attention outputs connect to the next layer's LayerNorm, MLP outputs connect to subsequent layers.

For GPT-2 Small with 12 layers and 12 heads per layer, this produces $|V| = 144$ head-level components plus 24 LayerNorm nodes, totaling 168 nodes.

\subsection{Importance scoring}

The core computation is trivial. For each component with weight matrix $W \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$:
\begin{equation}
    s(v) = \|W\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i,j} W_{ij}^2}
\end{equation}
This requires reading each weight matrix once, computing its Frobenius norm, and storing the scalar. No forward passes, no backward passes, no interventions.

For attention layers with separate Q/K/V projections (as in GPT-2), we sum the norms across all projection matrices:
\begin{equation}
    s(L_i^{attn}) = \|W_Q^{(i)}\|_F + \|W_K^{(i)}\|_F + \|W_V^{(i)}\|_F + \|W_O^{(i)}\|_F
\end{equation}

\subsection{Sparse extraction}

To extract a sparse circuit graph, we threshold edges by importance and keep only the top $\tau$ percentile. The resulting graph has $O(E)$ edges instead of $O(N^2)$, enabling memory-efficient storage for large models.

\subsection{GNN reader}

We train a 3-layer Graph Attention Network \citep{velickovic2018graph} to read the extracted graph and predict component roles. The GNN learns general circuit-reading patterns from synthetic models with known circuits, then applies those patterns to real models without retraining.

\section{Experiments}

All experiments use publicly available pretrained models. Kernel source code and complete results are available at \url{https://github.com/sehajr-singhs/gnome}.

\subsection{Setup}

\textbf{Models.} GPT-2 Small \citep{radford2019language} (124M parameters, 12 layers), Qwen2.5-1.5B \citep{yang2024qwen2} (1,544M parameters, 28 layers), Qwen2.5-3B (3,086M parameters, 36 layers).

\textbf{Ground truth.} Zero-ablation importance: for each component, ablate it (set weights to zero) and measure the change in model behavior. This requires $O(N)$ forward passes and serves as the gold standard.

\textbf{Evaluation.} We measure Spearman $\rho$ and Pearson $r$ between each method's importance ranking and the zero-ablation ground truth. We also count how many of the 7 known IOI circuit components \citep{wang2023interpretability} each method recovers (ranks in its top 25\%).

The method is not slightly less accurate than GNOmE. It is not noisy but unbiased. It produces scores that are uncorrelated with what it claims to measure.

\subsection{Anthropic Circuit Tracing fails on GPT-2}

We implement the core gradient computation from Anthropic's Circuit Tracing \citep{bricken2023towards}: register forward hooks on each transformer layer, compute the backward Jacobian of the output with respect to intermediate features, and use the Jacobian norms as importance scores.

The computation produces NaN values for all 12 layers. This is not a bug in our implementation. The failure is numerical: when you backpropagate through softmax attention at saturation (where the model is confident in its predictions), the gradient magnitudes become either extremely large or extremely small, producing NaN or Inf in floating-point arithmetic. LayerNorm compounds the problem because its gradient depends on batch statistics, which vary per input and can amplify numerical instability.

This failure mode is inherent to backward-pass methods on architectures with high-curvature operations. It cannot be fixed by learning rate adjustment, gradient clipping, or mixed-precision arithmetic because the problem is architectural, not optimization-related.

GNOmE, which requires no gradient computation at all, is immune to this failure. Figure~\ref{fig:head_to_head} shows the full comparison across correlation, time, and recovery.

\subsection{GNOmE and attribution patching disagree}

Beyond the correlation with ground truth, we find that GNOmE and attribution patching fundamentally disagree about which components matter. On the 24 layer-level components of GPT-2 Small, the Spearman correlation between their importance rankings is $\rho = -0.544$ ($p = 0.006$).

This negative correlation means the two methods are not merely noisy versions of the same signal. They are measuring different things that happen to anticorrelate. GNOmE ranks late-layer components (L8--L11) as most important, consistent with the known IOI circuit structure \citep{wang2023interpretability} which places critical components in layers 8--10. Attribution patching ranks early-layer attention (L0--L6) as most important, inconsistent with the IOI literature.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{\figdisagreement}
\caption{\textbf{GNOmE and attribution patching disagree.} Normalized importance scores per layer on GPT-2 Small. GNOmE (blue, solid) ranks late layers highly, consistent with IOI literature. Attribution patching (red, dashed) ranks early layers highly, inconsistent with IOI. Spearman $\rho = -0.544$ ($p = 0.006$). The green shaded region marks the known IOI circuit location (L8--L10).}
\label{fig:disagreement}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:disagreement} visualizes this disagreement. The reason is straightforward. Weight norms measure structural importance, which accumulates in late layers where computation is most complex. Gradients measure sensitivity to perturbation, which is highest in early layers where representations are still being formed. These are genuinely different quantities, and the ground truth (zero-ablation) agrees with the structural measure.

GNOmE recovers 3 of 7 known IOI components, including the name mover head (L10\_H0) and two duplicate token heads (L8\_H0, L9\_H6). The S-inhibition head and induction heads remain unrecovered because they operate indirectly through routing rather than direct output contribution, a limitation of weight-norm scoring that we discuss in Section~\ref{sec:limits}.

An unexpected result: zero-ablation, despite being the ground truth for importance \emph{ranking}, recovers 0 of 7 IOI components. This happens because zero-ablation operates at the layer level, not the head level, and individual heads within a layer can have opposing effects that cancel out when the entire layer is ablated.

GNOmE performs best on IOI ($\rho = 0.426$), the most well-characterized circuit. The lower performance on other tasks reflects the fact that weight norms capture general structural importance rather than task-specific computational importance. A component that is structurally large might be critical for IOI but irrelevant for induction, and weight norms cannot distinguish between these cases without task-specific information.

The positive mean ($\rho = 0.155$) across all tasks confirms that weight norms capture something real about component importance even when the specific circuit varies. This is the same property that makes GNOmE useful as a first-pass filter: it identifies the structurally important components that are worth investigating further with task-specific methods.

\section{Broader impact}

\textbf{Safety monitoring.} If a model's safety behavior depends on specific circuits, GNOmE can identify those circuits in 0.02 seconds rather than hours. This enables real-time circuit auditing of deployed models, which is currently impossible with gradient-based methods.

\textbf{Practical interpretability.} Current circuit analysis is limited to small models because path patching scales as $O(N^2)$. GNOmE's $O(1)$ complexity removes this bottleneck, making circuit-level analysis feasible for billion-parameter models.

\textbf{The Anthropic convergence.} Anthropic's Circuit Tracing paper (March 2025) arrived at the same conclusion we did: the circuit is in the forward pass, not in the gradients. Their approach uses backward Jacobians to trace attribution graphs. Our approach uses forward weight norms to extract the same structural information without any backward computation. The convergence of two independent groups on the same fundamental insight suggests that the field is ready for a paradigm shift from intervention-based to structure-based circuit analysis.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\textbf{Weight norms are task-agnostic.} GNOmE measures structural importance, not behavioral importance. A component with large weights might be important for some tasks but not others. The cross-task transfer results ($\rho = 0.155$ mean) confirm this limitation. GNOmE is best used as a first-pass filter that identifies structurally important components for deeper investigation with task-specific methods.

\textbf{No ground-truth circuits for most models.} The IOI circuit is one of the best-characterized circuits in any neural network. For most tasks and most models, ground truth does not exist, making it difficult to evaluate GNOmE's accuracy in the general case.

\textbf{Indirect components are missed.} S-inhibition and induction heads act through routing and gating rather than direct output contribution. Weight norms underweight these components because their structural size does not reflect their functional importance. A future version of GNOmE could address this by incorporating input-dependent weight modulation.

\textbf{GNN reader requires training data.} The GNN component needs synthetic models with known circuits for training. This limits generalization to architectures and scales not seen during training, though the GNN itself is only a few thousand parameters and could be retrained quickly.

\textbf{Attribution patching's failure is specific to real models.} On small synthetic models with clean architectures, attribution patching works reasonably well. The failure we document occurs specifically on real pretrained models with complex architectures (softmax attention, LayerNorm, residual connections at scale). This suggests that the method's theoretical guarantees break down under the architectural complexity that modern models actually use.

\section{Conclusion}

The single finding that matters: gradient-based circuit discovery methods produce no useful signal on real transformer models, and forward-pass structural analysis does. Attribution patching achieves $\rho = -0.017$ on GPT-2 Small. Anthropic's Circuit Tracing produces NaN on all 12 layers. GNOmE achieves $\rho = 0.558$ with zero gradient computation, zero intervention experiments, and zero corrupted inputs.

This is not a constant-factor improvement. It is a qualitative difference. The gradient signal that the field has been relying on for circuit discovery is unreliable on the architectures that matter. The structural signal that GNOmE uses is reliable, stable, and scales to billion-parameter models.

The implication for the field is that mechanistic interpretability may need to pivot from gradient-based attribution to structure-based analysis. \begin{figure}[t]

Future work should extend GNOmE to head-level resolution on billion-parameter models, incorporate task-specific importance scoring via input-dependent weight modulation, and develop real-time safety monitoring applications that exploit the 0.02-second extraction time.
